\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Models and protocol.}
We follow the protocol of \seacache~\cite{chung2026seacache}. For text-to-image generation we use
\flux~\cite{blackforestlabs2024flux} at $1024{\times}1024$ with 50 Euler steps of its default flow-matching schedule,
embedded guidance 3.5, and the 200 DrawBench prompts~\cite{saharia2022imagen}; the seed of each image is its prompt
index. For text-to-video generation we use HunyuanVideo~\cite{kong2024hunyuanvideo} at $480{\times}640$ with 65 frames
and Wan2.1-T2V-1.3B~\cite{wan2025wan} at $832{\times}480$ with 81 frames, both with 50 steps and seed 42, on the 944
unique VBench prompts~\cite{huang2024vbench}. All methods share the seeds, and each accelerated sample is compared with
the output of the uncached 50-step sampler for the same prompt and seed.

\paragraph{Budgets and baselines.}
All caches use the official \sea gate, and its threshold $\delta$ sets the budget. We report the mean number of
block-stack evaluations per image (\nfe, out of 50), counted by hooks on every block, and compare only at matched
\nfe, since the closed-loop gate can produce slightly different schedules for different fillers. On \flux,
$\delta=0.3$ and $0.6$ give 20.8 and 13.0 \nfe; at $\delta=0.6$ our run of \seacache reproduces its published PSNR
(21.26 vs.\ 21.33\,dB~\cite{chung2026seacache}). At these thresholds, \seacache, \kalsea and \ours computed exactly
the same steps for 188 and 182 of the 200 prompts, and the per-prompt \nfe never differs by more than one, so the
comparison isolates what is written on skipped steps. Behind the same gate we compare four fillers: reuse
(\seacache), linear extrapolation of the residual from the last two computed steps (the first-order finite-difference
forecast of TaylorSeer~\cite{liu2025taylorseer}), the Kalman forecast (\kalsea), and the Kalman forecast with \pcgs
(\ours).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
PSNR on 8-bit RGB, SSIM~\cite{wang2004ssim} (scikit-image, $7{\times}7$ window) and LPIPS (AlexNet
features~\cite{zhang2018lpips}) are computed against the 50-step reference and averaged over prompts. Differences
between methods come with paired bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals over prompts (10{,}000 resamples, percentile)
and the fraction of prompts on which a method is better.

\paragraph{Development and test prompts.}
All design decisions (the smoother, the re-integration and continuation rules, $\tau$ and $R$) were made on a
development set of 64 COCO captions~\cite{lin2014coco}, and the forecaster was calibrated on 16 other COCO captions.
DrawBench was only used to score finished configurations and is thus a held-out test set (development-set results:
supplement).

\paragraph{Implementation.}
For \flux, our cache replaces the forward pass of the transformer in diffusers; the scheduler is unchanged, and the
re-integration \eqref{eq:reint} reproduces its arithmetic. All runs use bf16 on NVIDIA B200 GPUs. For video we use the
per-element filter, since the DCT bands are defined on 2D token grids. \TODO{video implementation details}

\subsection{Comparison at matched \nfe}
\label{sec:main-results}

\cref{tab:flux-main} compares the fillers at the two budgets of the protocol. At 20.8 \nfe, \ours improves over
\seacache by 2.73\,dB PSNR (95\% CI [2.34, 3.12]; better on 88\% of the prompts), 0.031 SSIM [0.027, 0.036] and
0.033 LPIPS [0.029, 0.037]; at 13 \nfe, by 1.27\,dB [1.02, 1.52] (79\% of the prompts), 0.042 SSIM [0.035, 0.048] and
0.060 LPIPS [0.052, 0.068]. Both forecasters improve over reuse, and \pcgs adds 0.83\,dB [0.63, 1.02] at 20.8 \nfe and
0.61\,dB [0.48, 0.76] at 13 \nfe on top of \kalsea, with better PSNR on 81\% and 78\% of the prompts; \ours exceeds
linear extrapolation by 1.11\,dB [0.89, 1.34] and 0.73\,dB [0.54, 0.92]. The median per-prompt gain of \pcgs over
\kalsea is 0.60 and 0.44\,dB; on the remaining 19\% and 22\% of the prompts it lowers PSNR, by at most 3.6 and 2.3\,dB.

\paragraph{Budget sweep.}
\cref{fig:tradeoff} varies $\delta$ from 0.2 to 1.0. \ours attains the highest PSNR and the lowest LPIPS
at every budget. Its PSNR gain over \seacache grows with the budget, from 0.74\,dB [0.46, 1.04] at 11 \nfe to 3.89\,dB
[3.49, 4.28] at 27 \nfe; at 9 \nfe it is not significant (0.21\,dB [$-$0.005, 0.42]), whereas LPIPS still improves by
0.043 [0.033, 0.053]. The predictor alone behaves as the open-loop argument predicts: its gain over reuse shrinks from
2.37\,dB at 27 \nfe to 0.11\,dB [$-$0.14, 0.34] at 11 and $-$0.10\,dB [$-$0.31, 0.10] at 9 \nfe, and linear
extrapolation falls below reuse at 11 and 9 \nfe ($-$0.58 and $-$0.77\,dB), as forecast errors accumulate over long
gaps. \pcgs improves on \kalsea at every budget, by 0.31\,dB [0.24, 0.39] at 9 \nfe and up to
1.52\,dB [1.30, 1.75] at 27 \nfe.

\paragraph{Size of the correction.}
The relative latent correction $\|\lat'_b-\lat_b\|/\|\lat_b\|$ averages 0.7\% at 20.8 \nfe, where most skipped steps
lie in three-step gaps, and 2.0\% at 13 \nfe. It is larger after long and late gaps: at 13 \nfe, 0.2--0.3\% after the
single skipped steps early in sampling and 5--6\% after the last two long gaps of the most common schedule.

\paragraph{Qualitative results.}
\cref{fig:teaser} shows two prompts selected by a fixed rule. With the same computed steps, reuse alters
high-frequency structure (in the street scene, part of a facade turns into foliage); both forecasts restore it, and
\pcgs brings the image closest to the reference. The improvement is not uniform: for the cubes, the predictor alone is
closer to the reference than \ours. The supplement shows more examples at 13 and 20.8 \nfe.

\paragraph{Anatomy of the corrector.}
\cref{tab:ablation} reports paired PSNR differences to \ours. We do not interpret differences below 0.1\,dB: the
filter's band statistics are accumulated with atomic GPU additions, and a second run of \ours differs by
$+$0.02\,dB [$-$0.04, 0.08] and $+$0.04\,dB [$-$0.003, 0.10] at the two budgets. The gain comes from re-integrating the latent: feeding
the smoothed residuals only into the filter state is no better than no correction ($-$0.86 vs.\ $-$0.83\,dB at 20.8
\nfe). The shape of the gains matters little: a linear interpolation from $\res_a$ to $\res_b$, which ignores the
forecast, and the forecast plus a linearly weighted innovation match the RTS gains at 13 \nfe and exceed them by
0.14\,dB [0.04, 0.25] at 20.8 \nfe. We had chosen the RTS gains on the development set, where both were tied for the per-element filter
(interpolation minus RTS: $-$0.13\,dB [$-$0.31, 0.03] at 18 \nfe, $+$0.07\,dB [$-$0.05, 0.20] at 13 \nfe), and do not
switch based on the test set. Re-embedding the corrected latents matters at
the larger budget: the batched re-integration on the stored inputs $\hin_i$ loses 0.22\,dB [0.09, 0.34] at 20.8 \nfe
(0.06\,dB [$-$0.01, 0.13] at 13 \nfe). The gate refresh has no measurable effect.

\paragraph{Other forecasters and fillers.}
With \pcgs, the parametrization of the forecaster hardly matters: the per-element filter, the trend damping
$\tau=0.1$ of SpecKCast and a calibration-free per-element filter all stay within 0.1\,dB of \ours, and \pcgs adds
0.83\,dB [0.65, 1.01] and 0.58\,dB [0.44, 0.73] to the per-element filter, about as much as to the DCT filter. The frequency
decomposition of SpecKCast is thus unnecessary for the residual, and we use the per-element filter for video. Nor is
\pcgs tied to a Kalman forecaster: with the forecast plus a linearly weighted innovation as smoother, it improves
reuse by 1.69\,dB [1.51, 1.88] and 0.74\,dB [0.62, 0.86] and linear extrapolation by 1.15\,dB [0.92, 1.38] and
0.80\,dB [0.62, 0.98]. Linear extrapolation with \pcgs then ties \ours, while reuse with \pcgs stays 1.03 and
0.53\,dB below, so a forecasting predictor remains useful. Forecasting a sampler quantity (the clean-image, noise or
velocity prediction reconstructed from the current latent) instead of the residual is not better (supplement).
